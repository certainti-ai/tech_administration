Executive summary

CRA proposes to reduce every SR&ED figure on the claim to zero. Two projects were technically reviewed and found not eligible: the Research and Technology Advisor concluded no technological uncertainty existed and — more damagingly — that our written T661 narratives described experimentation the client's own team could not demonstrate in the room. Five projects were never reviewed for eligibility at all. They were denied purely because no salary breakdowns or contractor documentation were provided in answer to the request for information dated May 7, 2026.

These are two different failures and they require two different responses. Only one of them is recoverable, and even that one is conditional.

Section 02

What CRA is saying

Stripped of form language, the proposal package — the letter, the Project Eligibility Report signed July 16, the audit adjustment working papers and the schedules — makes four distinct assertions.

1

The reviewed work is engineering, not experimental development

For P1 and P2 the Advisor concluded the work was system modernisation, integration and configuration of existing technologies. The CDR simulation was read as quality assurance against known requirements — a “golden dataset” confirming that 72 attributes appeared correctly — rather than investigation of unknown behaviour. Kafka gaps, SFTP transfer delays and voucher-file decryption were read as access limitations solved with temporary scripts and workarounds. The verdict: no technological uncertainty, therefore no SR&ED under subsection 248(1).

2

Our written narratives were not backed up in the room

CRA flags explicitly, for both reviewed projects, a material mismatch between the section 244 project descriptions and what was demonstrated live. The narratives claimed timestamp-skew investigation, message-ordering analysis, Kafka runtime uncertainty, schema-agnostic parsers, checkpoint markers, telemetry injection, anomaly clustering, adaptive script logic and hypothesis-driven experimentation. None was evidenced in the interviews or in the documents submitted.

This is the most serious finding in the file. It is a credibility finding, not merely an eligibility finding, and it is why nothing we file may restate those mechanisms without a dated artefact behind it.

3

The documentation submitted proved nothing experimental

What CRA received was ten architecture and status slides for P1, and for P2 a KPI dashboard, tool screenshots and Confluence page captures. Both were assessed in writing as containing no evidence of an experiment, no experimental data, and no analysis of such data. Status decks and screenshots are not R&D evidence, and CRA has now said so on the record.

4

Five projects had no verifiable financial basis at all

For P3–P7 the May 7 request asked for salary breakdowns by employee, hours split between SR&ED and non-SR&ED work per project, and contractor support. A response was filed on June 4 covering P1 and P2. Nothing was submitted for P3–P7. CRA could not tell which employees worked on which project, how hours were allocated, whether contractors were arm's length, or whether ultimate performers were taxable suppliers. All $980,759 was disallowed as unsubstantiated, and all $304,752 of contractor cost across all seven projects was likewise unsupported.

What CRA is not saying

This is a proposal, not a reassessment. No penalty has been proposed. P3–P7 have not been found ineligible — they have been found undocumented, which is a different thing and a better starting point. CRA has left the door open: written representations by August 31, discussion with the Advisor and the Financial Reviewer and their managers, and Administrative Review for process concerns. The Notice of Objection right arises only after reassessment and is fully preserved.

The line 435 recapture, and why it is counter-intuitive

This is the hardest mechanic in the file and it will be misread otherwise. Line 435 deducts, from the SR&ED expenditure pool, the investment tax credits applied or refunded in the prior year. But CRA has simultaneously reduced allowable expenditures to nil, so the pool goes negative — and a negative pool is included in income.

The inclusion is contingent, and that matters commercially

The $175,850 is not a penalty and not an independent adjustment — it is the arithmetic consequence of denying this year's expenditures while a prior-year credit remains to be recovered. If any material part of FY2024 expenditure is restored, the pool turns positive again and the inclusion shrinks or disappears. So the value of contesting P3–P7 is the credits recovered plus the removal of this income inclusion. We have deliberately not put a figure on the second component: it depends on the deduction elected at line 460 in the restored scenario, and should be quantified with tax counsel once the substantiation position is known.

Test this, do not rely on it

The implied figures rest on an assumed 15% rate. If any part of the FY2023 credit was earned at a different rate, or if the reassessed figure reflects credits applied rather than credits earned, the numbers change. The FY2023 notice of reassessment settles it, and it is a single document.

The cross-year interaction, which cuts both ways

If an objection for FY2023 succeeds and the credit is restored toward $467,727, then — with FY2024 expenditures denied — the FY2024 line 435 deduction rises correspondingly, the negative pool deepens, and the FY2024 income inclusion rises from $175,850 toward $467,727. On a full restoration that is an additional $291,877 of FY2024 income, in the order of $77,000 of tax at a combined federal and Ontario rate of roughly 26.5%.

This is not an argument against objecting for FY2023

A full restoration recovers $291,877 of credit in FY2023 against roughly $77,000 of additional FY2024 tax. The net position is plainly favourable. It is an argument for running the two years together rather than independently, for making sure the client is not surprised by an FY2024 bill arriving on the back of an FY2023 win, and for ensuring our FY2024 representations assert nothing about the FY2023 credit that a live objection would contradict.

Section 09

Lessons learnt

Written for us, not for the client. Each one cost real money on this file.

1 · Narratives must never outrun the evidence

The finding that sank P1 and P2 was not “no uncertainty” — it was that our written descriptions claimed sophisticated hypothesis-driven experimentation the client's own SMEs could not demonstrate live and the documents did not show. A modest, defensible narrative beats an impressive, indefensible one every time. This applies with particular force to any AI-assisted or template-driven drafting in our own pipeline.

2 · A request for information ignored is a claim conceded

$980,759 across five projects was lost not on merit but because the May 7 salary and contractor requests went unanswered for P3–P7. There was a 28-day window in which routine payroll extracts could have kept these projects alive. Information requests need an owner, a deadline and a completeness sign-off like a production incident — because that is what they are.

3 · Screenshots are not evidence

Every supporting document submitted — status decks, dashboards, portal captures, Confluence pages — was operational collateral. CRA told us in writing what qualifies: experiments, experimental data, and analysis of that data. The evidence base must be commits, tickets, test logs and design records created during the work, not artefacts assembled at audit time.

4 · Interview readiness is a deliverable

Key technical personnel were unavailable for one review session, the discussion stayed at process level, and nobody in the room could walk the reviewer through the section 244 mechanisms. Review meetings decide claims. SMEs need prep sessions, the narrative in front of them and rehearsed evidence walk-throughs — or the meeting should be rescheduled.

5 · Managed-service claims carry structural risk

Large multi-shore service contracts — P2's 500-person model — invite exactly the reading CRA applied: coordination in Canada, execution elsewhere, engineering rather than experimentation. Claims built on such engagements need Canadian-performer identification and uncertainty framing settled at claim-preparation time, not at audit.

6 · A percentage is not an allocation

The whole salary claim was total project payroll times a flat 15%, and the contract claim was subcontractor cost times 15% times an unexplained 0.65. Nobody measured anything. The timesheets to do it properly existed the entire time, in a system with daily entries and an approval workflow — they were simply never used. The defect was not missing records. It was a method that never asked for them.

7 · Verify the other side's arithmetic, and verify your own

Two things came out of re-deriving this package rather than reading it. CRA's figures are clean, which tells us where not to spend effort. And our own best-case figure was overstated by roughly $14,000 — a number that had been circulating internally without anyone recomputing it. Check both directions before a number reaches a client.

Section 10

Process changes needed

Each change maps to a specific failure in this file, and each is encodable into the Think R&D 365 assessment workflow rather than left as policy text.

GATE 1
SME sign-off on every T661 narrative. No project description ships unless the named SME has reviewed it and confirmed, mechanism by mechanism, that they can defend every claimed activity live. Signature captured in-platform; unverified mechanisms get struck, not softened.
GATE 2
Artefact mapping before filing. Every claimed uncertainty and experiment must link to at least one dated contemporaneous artefact — repository, ticket, test log, design document — in an evidence register. Claims with unmapped mechanisms fail the platform's evidence-readiness score and do not proceed.
GATE 3
Financial substantiation pack assembled at claim time. Per-employee monthly hours split SR&ED and non-SR&ED per project code, reconciled to the T661 — CRA's own stated standard from this file — plus contractor agreements, invoices, and arm's-length and taxable-supplier confirmations, all archived with the claim before it is filed, not hunted after a request arrives.
GATE 4
Information-request response runbook. Any CRA request triggers a tracked case: owner, item-level checklist against the request text, completeness review, and escalation to practice leadership if any requested item will not be produced — with the decision documented, never defaulted.
GATE 5
Review-meeting protocol. Mandatory SME prep session, attendance confirmation for named technical personnel, evidence walk-through rehearsal, and a rule that meetings proceed only with the people who did the work in the room.
GATE 6
Portfolio audit of live claims. Immediate review of every unfiled and under-review claim across the practice for the same narrative-versus-evidence gap found here — same drafting process, same latent risk. Prioritise any claim using narrative templates shared with this file.
GATE 7
Multi-year account watch. Accounts with any prior-year adjustment get a heightened-scrutiny flag in the platform: conservative claim scoping, senior review of narratives, and a pre-filing evidence audit by default.
GATE 9
No flat-rate allocations, ever. New, from this file. A claim may not be built by applying a uniform percentage to project payroll. The SR&ED proportion must be derived per employee from what that person actually did, recorded during the year, and the platform should refuse to produce a claim where every employee on a project carries an identical rate.
GATE 8
Independent recomputation of every figure that reaches a client. New, from this week. Any number in a client-facing document — recovery estimate, credit at stake, scenario range — must be re-derived from source and reproducible, not carried forward from an earlier draft. The overstated best case on this file was caught only because someone recomputed it.
