Section 01

Executive summary

CRA proposes to reduce every SR&ED figure on the claim to zero. Two projects were technically reviewed and found not eligible. Five were never reviewed for eligibility at all — they were denied because the documentation requested on May 7, 2026 was not provided. Those are two different failures needing two different responses, and until this week we believed only the second was recoverable.

The client records have narrowed that further. The claim was not built from time records. It was built by applying a flat 15% to every employee's salary on six projects, and 50% on the seventh. The timesheets to do it properly existed throughout — 846,994 rows of daily, per-project, per-employee data in a system with a manager approval workflow. They were never used. A workbook in the client's audit folder also carries its monthly hour columns as #REF! errors for all 305 employees — but whether that particular file reached CRA is disputed and unresolved.

The honest headline

The ceiling is $250,795 of the $594,399 at stake, and reaching any part of it now requires building an allocation that was never built — employee by employee, as a present-day technical judgment on historic records, honestly labelled as that. Section 02

What CRA is saying

Four assertions, stripped of form language. Having now seen our own records, we can say something we could not say last week: all four are supported by them.

1

The reviewed work is engineering, not experimental development

For P1 and P2 the Advisor concluded the work was system modernisation, integration and configuration of existing technologies. The CDR simulation was read as quality assurance against known requirements rather than investigation of unknown behaviour. Kafka gaps, SFTP delays and voucher decryption were read as access limitations solved with workarounds. No technological uncertainty, therefore no SR&ED under subsection 248(1).

2

Our written narratives were not backed up in the room

CRA flags, for both reviewed projects, a mismatch between the section 244 descriptions and what was demonstrated. The narratives claimed timestamp-skew investigation, message-ordering analysis, Kafka runtime uncertainty, schema-agnostic parsers, checkpoint markers, telemetry injection, anomaly clustering and hypothesis-driven experimentation. None was evidenced.

We now know something that makes this worse rather than better. The hours quoted in those narratives are real — five of six reconcile exactly to the timesheet system. But they are total project hours, presented as investigative effort, against a claim asserting 15%. The narratives do not merely lack support; they contradict the company's own claim by roughly a factor of five.

3

The documentation submitted proved nothing experimental

Ten architecture and status slides for P1; a KPI dashboard, tool screenshots and Confluence captures for P2. Both assessed in writing as containing no evidence of an experiment, no experimental data, and no analysis of such data.

Having read the decks, the description is accurate. CRA's “Reference 5” is a ten-slide file whose contents are the E2E architecture, a SharePoint archive, test-plan approvals and four slides of daily status reports — exactly as described. The P2 walkthrough deck contains no images and no tables across nine slides.

4

Five projects had no verifiable financial basis at all

For P3–P7 the May 7 request asked for salary breakdowns by employee, hours split between SR&ED and non-SR&ED per project, and contractor support. A response was filed June 4 covering P1 and P2. Nothing was submitted for P3–P7. All $980,759 was disallowed as unsubstantiated, and all $304,752 of contractor cost across all seven projects likewise.

This is the finding our own records corroborate most completely. There was no SR&ED versus non-SR&ED distinction to submit, because none was ever made.

What CRA is not saying

This is a proposal, not a reassessment. No penalty has been proposed. P3–P7 have not been found ineligible — they have been found undocumented, which is a different and better starting point. The line 435 recapture, and why it is counter-intuitive

Line 435 deducts from the SR&ED pool the investment tax credits applied or refunded in the prior year. CRA reduced it from $467,727 to $175,850 because the FY2023 credit was itself cut on reassessment. Standing alone a smaller deduction is favourable. But CRA simultaneously reduced allowable expenditures to nil, so the pool goes negative — and a negative pool is included in income.

The inclusion is contingent, which matters commercially

It is not a penalty and not an independent adjustment — it is the arithmetic consequence of denying this year's expenditures while a prior-year credit remains to be recovered. If any material part of FY2024 expenditure is restored, the pool turns positive and the inclusion shrinks or disappears. The value of contesting P3–P7 is therefore the credits plus the removal of this inclusion. We have not put a figure on the second component: it depends on the deduction elected at line 460 in the restored scenario, and should be quantified with tax counsel.

Section 12

Lessons learnt

Written for us, not for the client. Each one cost real money on this file.

1 · A percentage is not an allocation

The entire salary claim was project payroll times a flat 15%, and the contract claim was subcontractor cost times 15% times an unexplained 0.65. Nobody measured anything. The timesheets to do it properly existed the whole time, in a system with daily entries and an approval workflow. The defect was not missing records. It was a method that never asked for them.

2 · Narratives must never outrun the evidence

The finding that sank P1 and P2 was that our written descriptions claimed sophisticated experimentation the client's own SMEs could not demonstrate. Worse, we now know the narratives quoted every hour booked to each project as investigative effort while the claim asserted 15%. A modest, defensible narrative beats an impressive, indefensible one every time — and it must be consistent with the financial claim filed alongside it.

3 · Never send a spreadsheet you have not opened — and keep a copy of exactly what you sent

A workbook in the audit folder, Financial details for June 7 2026 response.xlsx, carries 3,660 #REF! cells across the twelve monthly hour columns for all 305 employees — precisely the item the request asked for — and the August 24 revision still has not fixed them. Whether that file went to CRA is disputed. That we cannot answer the question from our own records is the lesson: the submission set was never archived as sent, so a month later nobody can say with certainty which workbook the Agency holds.

4 · An information request ignored is a claim conceded

$980,759 across five projects was lost not on merit but because the May 7 requests went unanswered for P3–P7. Information requests need an owner, a deadline and a completeness sign-off like a production incident — because that is what they are.

5 · Screenshots are not evidence, and neither is a walkthrough deck

Every supporting document submitted was operational collateral. The P2 walkthrough had nine slides, no images and no tables, including one titled “Supporting Evidence” that described evidence instead of being it. CRA told us in writing what qualifies: experiments, experimental data, and analysis of that data.

6 · Check that a contract covers the year you are claiming

Three of the eight subcontractor agreements begin after FY2024 ended, one of them after the audit started, and the largest vendor's agreement is between a US affiliate and a US entity. The payment trail is excellent. Nobody checked whether the paperwork underneath it covered the right period or named the right parties.

7 · Managed-service claims carry structural risk

Large multi-shore service contracts invite exactly the reading CRA applied: coordination in Canada, execution elsewhere. P2 had 169 employee rows, 46 of them unnamed, and not one hour booked in the Canadian time system. Claims built on such engagements need Canadian-performer identification settled at claim-preparation time.

8 · Verify the other side's arithmetic, and verify your own

Two things came from re-deriving this package rather than reading it. CRA's figures are clean, which tells us where not to spend effort. And our own best-case figure was overstated by roughly $14,000 — a number that had circulated internally without anyone recomputing it. Check both directions before a number reaches a client.

Section 13

Process changes needed

Each maps to a specific failure in this file, and each is encodable into the Think R&D 365 assessment workflow rather than left as policy text.

GATE 1
No flat-rate allocations, ever. A claim may not be built by applying a uniform percentage to project payroll. The SR&ED proportion must be derived per employee from what that person actually did, recorded during the year. The platform should refuse to produce a claim where every employee on a project carries an identical rate.
GATE 2
Time data at claim time, not audit time. Where a time system exists, the claim is built from it. Booked hours, per employee, per project, per month, with the SR&ED proportion captured against the work as it happens — not reconstructed from capacity two years later.
GATE 3
SME sign-off on every T661 narrative. No description ships unless the named SME confirms, mechanism by mechanism, that they can defend every claimed activity live. Unverified mechanisms get struck, not softened.
GATE 4
Narrative and financials must agree. If the narrative quotes hours, they must be the hours claimed — not the project total. A cross-check between the section 244 text and the financial workbook before filing would have caught the fivefold discrepancy in this file.
GATE 5
Artefact mapping before filing. Every claimed uncertainty must link to at least one dated contemporaneous artefact in an evidence register. Claims with unmapped mechanisms fail the evidence-readiness score and do not proceed.
GATE 6
Contract evidence checked for period and parties. For every contract claim: an agreement whose term covers the claim year, naming the claimant and the actual vendor, plus invoices and proof of payment. A payment trail alone is not support.
GATE 7
Nothing leaves without being opened. Every file sent to the CRA is opened and read by a second person first. Formula errors, broken references and stale tabs are checked as a release gate.
GATE 8
Information-request runbook. Any CRA request triggers a tracked case: owner, item-level checklist against the request text, completeness review, and escalation if any item will not be produced — the decision documented, never defaulted.
GATE 9
Review-meeting protocol. Mandatory SME prep, attendance confirmation for named technical personnel, evidence walk-through rehearsal, and a rule that meetings proceed only with the people who did the work in the room.
GATE 10
Portfolio audit and multi-year watch. Immediate review of every live claim for the same narrative-versus-evidence and flat-rate patterns found here. Accounts with any prior-year adjustment get a heightened-scrutiny flag: conservative scoping, senior narrative review, pre-filing evidence audit.
GATE 11
Independent recomputation of every figure that reaches a client. Any number in a client-facing document must be re-derived from source and reproducible, not carried forward from an earlier draft.
